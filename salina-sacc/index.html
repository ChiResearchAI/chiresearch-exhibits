Common Operating Picture — role-based demonstration

Every figure on this page expands to its definition, its source and its exclusions. That is the point of the exhibit: a Common Operating Picture is not a screen, it is an agreement about what each number means, and the agreement has to be visible to the person reading it.

The executive view answers three questions and stops: does the airfield meet the standard it is held to, is weather exposure changing, and is any number on this page one the Authority could not defend if asked.

Definition & source
Definition
Share of hourly observations in which the crosswind component on RWY 17/35 does not exceed 10.5 knots. Crosswind = wind speed × |sin(wind direction − runway true heading)|.
Standard
FAA AC 150/5300-13B Change 1, Airport Design (active, issued 16 Aug 2024; supersedes 13A, cancelled 31 Mar 2022). 10.5 kt is the allowable crosswind for Runway Design Code A-I and B-I.
Source
Iowa Environmental Mesonet ASOS archive, station SLN, hourly routine observations, 1 Jan 2016 – 31 Dec 2025 UTC.
Population
87,328 records retrieved; 85,824 used; 1,504 excluded for missing wind direction or speed. Calm observations (6,185, 7.21%) are counted as covered on every runway.
Known limits
Runway true headings are derived from the FAA magnetic designators plus the published 7°E variation, not from surveyed bearings. A production implementation would source runway geometry from the Authority's electronic ALP. See the governance card below.

Definition & source
Definition
An observation is below VFR when ceiling is under 3,000 ft or visibility is 5 statute miles or less. Ceiling is the lowest BKN, OVC or VV layer.
Why this is a governance example
Counting by ceiling alone gives 9,587 hours. Counting by visibility alone gives 2,345. Counting by either gives 11,331. All three are defensible; they differ by more than 4×. Until one is certified, "how much bad weather did we have" has no single answer.

One governance finding, shown rather than asserted

What a single unstated convention does to a reported number

METAR wind direction is true. FAA runway designators are magnetic. At SLN the difference is 7°. An integration that compares them directly — a quiet, ordinary mistake — changes this result:

RWY 17/35 wind coverage at 10.5 knots, corrected and uncorrected
RWY 17/35 at 10.5 ktCoverageAgainst 95%
Corrected for 7°E variation94.74%Under
Uncorrected95.29%Over

Same ten years, same 85,824 observations, same formula. Only the heading convention differs, and the verdict against an FAA standard reverses. Neither screen would look wrong.

Why this is in an executive view
The point
Because the failure is silent. A dashboard cannot tell leadership that a number is wrong; it can only tell them where the number came from. That is why runway geometry, magnetic variation and metric definitions belong in a governed semantic layer sourced from the Authority's ALP, and why establishing it is Phase 1 work rather than a configuration detail deferred to Phase 2.
Direction of the error
It is not uniform. The same uncorrected comparison moves RWY 12/30 the other way, from 84.20% to 81.64%. An error that moved every number the same way would be easier to notice.

The operations view is built around a single question an operations duty officer actually asks: which runway is usable, for which aircraft, and how often does that change.

Definition & source
Runway shading
All-weather wind coverage for that runway alone, at the selected allowable crosswind component, over 85,824 hourly observations (2016–2025). Green where coverage meets or exceeds the 95% recommended in AC 150/5300-13B Change 1; amber where it falls below. Shading never carries the verdict by itself — every runway is labelled with its figure and its status in words.
Heading convention
METAR wind direction is true; FAA runway designators are magnetic. Published variation at SLN is 7°E. Corrected converts the designator to a true bearing before computing the crosswind, which is the correct method. Uncorrected compares the magnetic designator directly against true wind — the quiet, ordinary mistake. Both are shown because the choice changes the verdict on Runway 17/35 and a reader cannot see which convention a dashboard used.
What this is not
A prototype built for this submittal, not a deployed system and not a prior client implementation. It demonstrates that the Authority’s airfield can be stood up as interactive GIS from public data in Phase 1, before any Authority system is touched.

Salina Regional is a Part 139 certificated airport, and the obstacle environment around it is published by the FAA. This view takes that published data and asks the question a Common Operating Picture has to get right: which number would a dashboard put on a screen, and would it be the true one?

Correct arithmetic, wrong answer
Definition & source
Definition
Share of the population whose FAA lighting code is N (none) or U (unknown). Note that N and U are different facts: one says there is no light, the other says the FAA does not record one. Collapsing them is itself a definition decision, and it is made visible here rather than buried.

Obstacles requiring lighting that lack it

At or above 200 ft AGL, within 5 NM

—
Every obstacle at or above the height at which FAA marking and lighting standards normally apply is lit. The tallest unlit object stands at 199 ft.
The number worth acting on
Definition & source
Threshold
FAA guidance is that a structure exceeding 200 ft above ground level, or exceeding an obstruction standard in 14 CFR Part 77, should normally be marked and lighted — AC 70/7460-1M, Obstruction Marking and Lighting, effective 16 November 2020. The FAA may also recommend marking or lighting below 200 ft because of a structure's particular location, so this threshold is a filter for reading the file, not a safety determination.
What this is not
Not an airspace or obstruction evaluation, and not a determination that any structure is or is not a hazard. Those are the FAA's to make.

Finding 4 · the same failure as the heading convention, in a different file

Within 5 NM of the airport, — of — obstacles carry a lighting value of “none” or “unknown”. A dashboard that charts that field reports —% of the obstacle environment unlit, which reads as an airfield in trouble.

It is the wrong reading, and the data says so plainly. The tallest object in that entire unlit population stands at 199 feet — not one of them reaches the 200-foot height at which FAA marking and lighting standards normally apply. The population is poles, signs, fences and low buildings, and its median height is 44 feet. Meanwhile every one of the — obstacles at or above 200 ft AGL within 5 NM is lit.

Widen to 10 NM and exactly one obstacle at or above 200 ft AGL carries a lighting code of “none”: a 250-foot tower at 5.5 NM, FAA-verified. That is the item an operations screen should surface — one, not three hundred. The discipline that produces the second answer instead of the first is the same discipline that caught the heading convention in Finding 2: read the field's definition before you aggregate it.

What drives an inspection here

14 CFR §139.327, self-inspection program

Daily
Except as otherwise required by the Airport Certification Manual.
On unusual condition
When a condition such as construction or weather may affect safe air carrier operations.
After an accident or incident
Immediately.
Records
Each inspection recorded with the conditions found and all corrective actions taken, retained at least 12 consecutive calendar months.

The Authority already holds these records in App139. Nothing in this view reads them, and no inspection record appears anywhere on this page. The point is that the cadence above is what a maintenance and operations view has to be built around, and the obstacle environment on the left is one of the things an inspection regime exists to watch.
